· ABOUT CHENGDU SHADOW PUPPETRY

Chengdu shadow puppetry is a distinctive branch of the Sichuan shadow theatre tradition, integrating carving, painting, theatre, and music into a single performance form. With a history of more than two thousand years, it was inscribed on the UNESCO Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2011 (UNESCO, 2011).

Its figures are notably larger and more expressive than many northern traditions, shaped by the influence of Sichuan Opera and built from around fourteen movable joints that allow flexible, lifelike movement. The craftsmanship is marked by intricate openwork carving, recurring motifs such as dragon and plum blossom patterns, and a highly recognisable facial style (Chengdu Museum, 2020; Marshall, 2022).

Rather than following the sheng, dan, jing, mo, and chou role categories of Chinese opera, character identity here is built through detachable, reconfigurable components — heads, headwear, costumes, and props. This modular design forms a stable and distinctive visual symbol system (Chengdu Museum, 2020).

The Visual Symbol System

Chengdu shadow puppet characters, in the museum's archive, are not actors — they are assembled objects. Each figure is built from a set of detachable, interchangeable parts: a carved head, a separate headwear piece, and a jointed body with roughly fourteen movable points. Identity is not performed through costume and gesture on stage, but constructed piece by piece before the performance even begins.

The figure below shows exactly how this works. Each part — from the helmet's crest to the boots — carries its own layer of meaning, and can, in principle, be swapped onto a different body without losing what it signifies.
