01 · ABOUT CHENGDU SHADOW PUPPETRY

Chengdu shadow puppetry, also known as Chengdu shadow theatre, is one of the most distinctive regional traditions within Chinese shadow puppetry and represents an important branch of the Sichuan shadow puppetry tradition (Chengdu Museum, 2020). As a traditional Chinese folk art, shadow puppetry integrates carving, painting, theatre, music, and folk customs into a single performance form. With a history of more than two thousand years, it was inscribed on the UNESCO Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2011 (UNESCO, 2011). Beyond its artistic value, shadow puppetry is regarded as an important carrier of Chinese traditional culture, preserving local history, folk beliefs, collective memory, and traditional craftsmanship while reflecting the aesthetic values and cultural identity of Chinese society.

The mature form of Chengdu shadow puppetry can be traced back to the middle and late Qing Dynasty, although its artistic and technical foundations originated much earlier, drawing upon the development of leather processing, carving techniques, and Chinese opera traditions from the Han and Tang dynasties (Chengdu Museum, 2020). Through its long historical development, Chengdu shadow puppetry gradually established a distinctive regional style that differs from other major traditions, such as those of Shaanxi and Tangshan. Shadow figures are generally larger in scale, with exaggerated body proportions strongly influenced by Sichuan Opera. Each figure usually consists of approximately fourteen movable joints, allowing more flexible and expressive movements than many northern shadow puppets (Chengdu Museum, 2020).

In terms of craftsmanship, Chengdu shadow puppetry is characterised by intricate openwork carving and the extensive use of decorative motifs, including dragon, python, plum blossom, and snowflake patterns. The facial design is distinguished by a high forehead, bulbous nose, slightly raised upper lip, and receding chin, creating a highly recognisable regional style (Marshall, 2022). In addition, the thicker cattle hide used in Chengdu led to the development of a distinctive chiselling technique, in which craftsmen carved dense and delicate patterns using small chisels and hammers (Chengdu Museum, 2020). Chengdu shadow puppetry also differs from many northern traditions in its preference for blue rather than green as a dominant colour, a characteristic that may be associated with the local tradition of blue printed textiles (Chengdu Museum, 2020). Together, these features give Chengdu shadow puppetry a distinctive visual language and considerable value for visual design research.

Unlike traditional Chinese opera, where characters are primarily classified according to the role categories of sheng, dan, jing, mo, and chou, character identity in Chengdu shadow puppetry is mainly communicated through the modular combination of detachable components, including heads, headwear, costumes, and props (Chengdu Museum, 2020). This detachable and reconfigurable visual system forms a relatively stable visual symbol system, making Chengdu shadow puppetry not only an important example of Chinese folk art but also a valuable case for the study of visual communication, digital preservation, and interactive cultural heritage presentation.
